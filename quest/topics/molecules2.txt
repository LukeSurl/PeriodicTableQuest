Which of these can molecular orbital theory explain, but the Lewis model can't?
Elements: O
- Why H₂ has a single bond
- Why F₂ has three lone pairs on each atom
* Why O₂ is paramagnetic
- Why HF is polar
# Explanation: Paramagnetism needs unpaired electrons, which the Lewis structure of O₂ doesn't have. The others are all fine in the Lewis model.

Which bonding theory uses orbitals that can spread out over the entire molecule?
- Valence bond theory
* Molecular orbital theory
- The Lewis model
- None of them: orbitals always belong to one atom
# Explanation: Valence bond theory builds tidy bonds from overlapping orbitals on neighbouring atoms. MO theory is more quantitative and lets orbitals span the whole molecule.

What is the orbital approximation?
- Molecular orbitals are made by adding and subtracting atomic orbitals
- Each electron stays in an orbital belonging to its own atom
* The molecule's overall electronic state is a product of one wavefunction for each electron
- Only the core electrons need to be considered
# Explanation: Ψ(molecule) = ψ(electron 1) × ψ(electron 2) × … Each single-electron wavefunction ψ is a molecular orbital. Adding and subtracting atomic orbitals is the other approximation, LCAO.

In the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) approach, how are molecular orbitals made?
- By multiplying together one wavefunction per electron
- By hybridising the orbitals of one atom
* By adding and subtracting atomic orbitals
- By counting bonding pairs
# Explanation: Adding gives an in-phase combination; subtracting gives an out-of-phase combination.

Adding the 1s orbitals of two hydrogen atoms (an in-phase combination) gives…
Elements: H
* A bonding orbital, with a lot of electron density between the nuclei
- An antibonding orbital, with a nodal plane between the nuclei
- A 2s orbital on one of the atoms
- Nothing: the two orbitals cancel out
# Explanation: The wavefunctions reinforce each other between the nuclei. Electron density there attracts both nuclei and holds the molecule together.

Subtracting the 1s orbitals of two hydrogen atoms (an out-of-phase combination) gives…
Elements: H
* An antibonding orbital, with a nodal plane halfway between the nuclei
- A bonding orbital, with a lot of electron density between the nuclei
- A second bonding orbital of the same energy
- Nothing: you can only add orbitals
# Explanation: One half of the orbital is positive and the other negative, so ψ is zero on the plane where they meet.

In the antibonding orbital of H₂, what is the probability of finding the electron on the plane exactly halfway between the nuclei?
- Higher than anywhere else
- The same as for the bonding orbital
* Zero
- 50%
# Explanation: That is the nodal plane: ψ = 0 there, so ψ² = 0 too.

Why does an electron in an antibonding orbital weaken a bond?
- It has the opposite spin to the bonding electrons
- It repels the bonding electrons out of the molecule
* Its density is mostly outside the region between the nuclei, pulling the atoms apart
- It is shared by only one of the atoms
# Explanation: With little density between the nuclei, the nuclei are pulled outwards rather than together.

Two atomic orbitals combine. How many molecular orbitals are formed?
- 1
* 2
- 4
- It depends on how many electrons there are
# Explanation: The number of molecular orbitals always equals the number of atomic orbitals combined: one in-phase and one out-of-phase combination.

The six 2pz orbitals of the carbon atoms in benzene's ring combine. How many molecular orbitals form?
Elements: C
- 3
- 2
* 6
- 12
# Explanation: There are six different ways of combining them, one molecular orbital for each atomic orbital that went in.

Compared with the 1s orbitals of separate H atoms, where are the H₂ molecular orbitals on an energy diagram?
Elements: H
* The bonding orbital is lower, and the antibonding orbital higher by about the same amount
- Both are lower
- Both are higher
- Both are at the same energy as the 1s orbitals
# Explanation: A lower-energy bonding orbital matches H + H → H₂ releasing energy. (The antibonding orbital is raised slightly more, but you can ignore that.)

What is the most electrons a single molecular orbital can hold?
- 1
- 2, with the same spin
* 2, with opposite spins
- 4
# Explanation: The Pauli exclusion principle applies to molecular orbitals just as to atomic ones. Lower-energy orbitals are filled first.

Which orbital do both electrons of H₂ occupy?
Elements: H
- 1σᵤ*, the antibonding orbital
- One in each
- The 1s orbital of one hydrogen atom
* 1σg, the bonding orbital
# Explanation: Both go into the lower, bonding orbital with opposite spins: that is what holds H₂ together.

Why doesn't He₂ exist?
Elements: He
* Its 2 bonding and 2 antibonding electrons cancel out: bond order 0
- Helium atoms are too small for their orbitals to overlap
- Helium has no electrons to share
- Its electrons all go into the antibonding orbital
# Explanation: The extra two electrons have to go into the antibonding orbital, which undoes the effect of the bonding pair.

In molecular orbital theory, how is bond order calculated?
* (Electrons in bonding orbitals − electrons in antibonding orbitals) ÷ 2
- Electrons in bonding orbitals − electrons in antibonding orbitals
- Electrons in bonding orbitals ÷ 2
- (Electrons in bonding orbitals + electrons in antibonding orbitals) ÷ 2
# Explanation: Some sources use filled bonding orbitals − filled antibonding orbitals, counting a half-filled orbital as ½. It gives the same answer.

For a molecule to be stable, its bond order must be…
- At least 1
- A whole number
* Greater than 0
- Exactly 1
# Explanation: Even a bond order of ½ (as in H₂⁺) gives a stable, if weak, bond.

H₂⁺ and H₂⁻ both have a bond order of ½. Compared with H₂, they…
Elements: H
* Have weaker bonds, and are paramagnetic
- Have stronger bonds, and are paramagnetic
- Have weaker bonds, and are not paramagnetic
- Can't exist
# Explanation: Lower bond order, lower dissociation energy. Each has an odd number of electrons, so one is unpaired.

Why can we usually ignore lithium's 1s electrons when building the MO diagram of Li₂?
Elements: Li
- 1s orbitals can't combine with each other
- They are lost when Li₂ forms
* They overlap very little, and their filled bonding and antibonding orbitals cancel out
- They are part of the nucleus
# Explanation: Core orbitals sit close to their own nucleus, so the core bonding and antibonding orbitals barely shift in energy, and with both full their effects cancel. Only the valence 2s orbitals matter.

H₂ and Li₂ both have a bond order of 1, but H₂'s bond is far stronger (436 vs 105 kJ mol⁻¹). Why?
Elements: Li
* Hydrogen's compact 1s orbitals overlap more than lithium's diffuse 2s orbitals
- H₂ has more bonding electrons
- The core electrons of Li₂ are in antibonding orbitals and weaken it
- Lithium is a metal, so it can't bond covalently
# Explanation: Weaker overlap means the bonding orbital is lowered less, so less energy is released when the bond forms. The core electrons cancel out, so they don't explain it.
