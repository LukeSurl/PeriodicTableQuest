In a diatomic molecule, which p orbital is, by convention, the one pointing towards the other atom?
* pz
- pₓ
- py
- All three point towards it equally
# Explanation: The internuclear axis is called z, so pz points along it. pₓ and py are at right angles to it.

Why is the axis between the two nuclei of a diatomic called "z"?
- Because z is always vertical
- Because pz is lower in energy than pₓ and py
- Because the electrons orbit around the z axis
* It is just a convention
# Explanation: There are no built-in x, y and z directions in the universe. Calling the bond axis z simply makes the labels consistent.

Two pz orbitals overlap end-on, along the internuclear axis. What type of molecular orbitals do they form?
* σ bonding and σ antibonding
- π bonding and π antibonding
- Only a σ bonding orbital
- Non-bonding orbitals
# Explanation: End-on overlap gives σ orbitals, just like s–s overlap. In phase gives the bonding orbital, out of phase the antibonding one.

Two pₓ orbitals, at right angles to the internuclear axis, overlap side-on. What type of molecular orbitals do they form?
- σ bonding and σ antibonding
* π bonding and π antibonding
- Nothing: they don't point at each other
- Only a π antibonding orbital
# Explanation: Side-on overlap gives π orbitals, with electron density above and below the axis.

Which overlap is stronger: end-on (pz with pz) or side-on (pₓ with pₓ)?
- Side-on (π)
- They are the same
* End-on (σ)
- It depends on the element
# Explanation: pz orbitals point straight at each other, so they overlap far more than two parallel pₓ orbitals do.

Because π overlap is weaker than σ overlap, the π bonding and antibonding orbitals of F₂ are…
Elements: F
- Further from the original 2p orbitals than the σ ones are
* Closer in energy to the original 2p orbitals than the σ ones are
- Both below the original 2p orbitals
- At exactly the same energies as the σ orbitals
# Explanation: Weaker overlap means a smaller bonding effect and a smaller antibonding effect. So 1πᵤ sits above 3σg, and 1πg* below 3σᵤ*.

In a homonuclear diatomic, why doesn't the pₓ orbital on one atom form bonding and antibonding orbitals with the pz orbital on the other?
- The pₓ orbital is higher in energy
* The in-phase and out-of-phase parts of the overlap cancel, so there is no net interaction
- They are too far apart
- Only orbitals with the same letter can ever combine
# Explanation: Half of the pₓ orbital overlaps the pz lobe in phase and half out of phase. For the same reason, in a diatomic pₓ and py can't interact with an s orbital on the other atom.

What do σ orbitals have that π orbitals don't?
- A nodal plane between the nuclei
* Rotational symmetry about the internuclear axis
- Electron density above and below the axis
- Room for more than two electrons
# Explanation: Spin a σ orbital about the bond axis and it looks the same. A π orbital has its density above and below the axis, so it doesn't.

What does the star in a label such as σᵤ* tell you?
* It is an antibonding orbital
- It is the highest occupied orbital
- It contains unpaired electrons
- It was formed by side-on overlap
# Explanation: Antibonding orbitals are marked with stars, though some sources leave them off.

What do the g and u in σg and πᵤ stand for?
- Ground state and upper state
* Gerade (even) and ungerade (uneven): how the orbital behaves under inversion
- Bonding and antibonding
- Gained and unchanged electrons
# Explanation: Inverting through the centre of the molecule leaves the phase of a g orbital unchanged and flips that of a u orbital. These labels are only used for bonds between identical atoms.

In a homonuclear diatomic, what does the 3 in 3σg mean?
- It holds three electrons
- It is made from n = 3 atomic orbitals
* It is the third σg orbital in energy order, counting those from the core
- It is the third orbital in the diagram overall
# Explanation: In F₂, 1σg comes from the 1s core orbitals, 2σg from 2s and 3σg from 2pz.

In a homonuclear diatomic, when molecular orbitals are numbered, how do the two degenerate πᵤ orbitals count?
* Once: both are 1πᵤ
- Twice: 1πᵤ and 2πᵤ
- Not at all: only σ orbitals are numbered
- As half each
# Explanation: A degenerate pair counts as one step in the sequence, so the next set of πᵤ orbitals would be 2πᵤ.

The Lewis model gives F₂ a single shared pair. How many valence electrons does molecular orbital theory place in the molecular orbitals of F₂?
Elements: F
- 2
* 14
- 8
- 12
# Explanation: Seven from each fluorine. Eight are in bonding orbitals and six in antibonding orbitals, so the bond order is still (8 − 6) ÷ 2 = 1: six electrons are trying to pull the molecule apart.

According to molecular orbital theory, why is O₂ paramagnetic?
Elements: O
- It has one electron in 3σᵤ*
- Its 3σg orbital is only half full
* Its two highest-energy electrons sit singly, with parallel spins, in the two degenerate 1πg* orbitals
- O₂ is really an ion
# Explanation: O₂ has 12 valence electrons; the last two go into 1πg*, one in each orbital (Hund's rule). The Lewis model can't explain this.

Molecular orbital theory gives O₂ a bond order of 2. Which electrons does that count?
Elements: O
* 8 in bonding orbitals and 4 in antibonding orbitals
- 4 in bonding orbitals and 0 in antibonding orbitals
- 12 in bonding orbitals and 8 in antibonding orbitals
- 6 in bonding orbitals and 2 in antibonding orbitals
# Explanation: 2σg² 2σᵤ*² 3σg² 1πᵤ⁴ 1πg*²: bonding 2 + 2 + 4 = 8, antibonding 2 + 2 = 4. (8 − 4) ÷ 2 = 2.

What is the HOMO of a molecule?
* The highest-energy molecular orbital that contains electrons
- The lowest-energy molecular orbital
- The highest-energy bonding orbital
- The lowest orbital with room for another electron
# Explanation: Its electrons are the ones most likely to be lost in a chemical or physical reaction. It may be bonding or antibonding, full or partly filled.

What is the LUMO of a molecule?
- The highest-energy molecular orbital
- The lowest-energy antibonding orbital
* The lowest-energy molecular orbital with room for another electron
- The highest orbital that contains electrons
# Explanation: This is where an electron goes if the molecule gains one. It may be bonding or antibonding, empty or partly filled.

Can the HOMO of a molecule be an antibonding orbital?
Elements: F
- No: the HOMO is always a bonding orbital
- Only in ions
* Yes: in F₂, for example, the HOMO is 1πg*
- Only if the molecule doesn't exist
# Explanation: In F₂ the highest occupied orbitals are the antibonding 1πg* pair, and its LUMO is 3σᵤ*.

Which orbital of O₂ is both its HOMO and its LUMO?
Elements: O
- 1πᵤ
* 1πg*
- 3σg
- 3σᵤ*
# Explanation: The 1πg* pair holds only two of its possible four electrons: it has the highest-energy electrons and also the lowest-energy vacancies.
