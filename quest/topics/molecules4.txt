When an electron is removed from a diatomic molecule, what decides whether the bond order goes up or down?
* Whether the HOMO it comes from is a bonding or an antibonding orbital
- Whether the molecule is homonuclear or heteronuclear
- Whether the electron was paired
- Removing an electron always lowers the bond order
# Explanation: Losing a bonding electron lowers the bond order by ½ (N₂ → N₂⁺); losing an antibonding electron raises it by ½ (O₂ → O₂⁺).

O₂⁺ has a shorter, stronger bond than O₂ (112 pm and 643 kJ mol⁻¹, against 120.7 pm and 498.5 kJ mol⁻¹). Why?
Elements: O
- The electron removed came from a bonding orbital, so the bond order rises
* The electron removed came from an antibonding 1πg* orbital, so the bond order rises to 2½
- Positive ions always have stronger bonds
- O₂⁺ has more electrons in bonding orbitals
# Explanation: 8 bonding and 3 antibonding electrons: (8 − 3) ÷ 2 = 2½.

Which molecular orbitals can interact with each other in s–p mixing?
- Any σ orbital with any π orbital
* σ orbitals with the same symmetry label (σg with σg, σᵤ with σᵤ)
- σg with σᵤ
- Only the core orbitals
# Explanation: The σ orbitals from s–s overlap and from pz–pz overlap can interact, but only if they share a symmetry label.

When two σ molecular orbitals interact through s–p mixing, what happens to their energies?
* The lower one is pushed further down and the higher one further up
- Both are pushed up
- Both are pushed down
- They end up at the same energy
# Explanation: The lower orbital is stabilised and the higher one destabilised. In N₂ this pushes 3σg up above 1πᵤ.

What makes s–p mixing between two σ molecular orbitals stronger?
- The σ orbitals being further apart in energy
- More electrons in the molecule
* The σ orbitals being closer in energy
- The atoms being further apart
# Explanation: The closer in energy the σ molecular orbitals are, the greater the effect.

Why could we ignore s–p mixing for O₂ and F₂?
Elements: F
- O and F have no 2s electrons
- O₂ and F₂ have no σ orbitals
- Their 2p orbitals are already full
* The 2s–2p energy gap grows across the period, so by O and F the σ orbitals are too far apart to mix much
# Explanation: Some mixing still happens in O₂, but not enough to push 3σg above 1πᵤ.

Why don't the σ orbitals made from the 1s core orbitals take part in s–p mixing?
- They have the wrong symmetry label
* There is a huge energy gap between them and the σ orbitals from 2s and 2p
- They are empty
- Core orbitals don't form molecular orbitals at all
# Explanation: The bigger the energy gap, the weaker the mixing, and here it is essentially zero.

Which orbital is the HOMO of N₂?
Elements: N
- 1πᵤ
* 3σg
- 1πg*
- 2σᵤ*
# Explanation: s–p mixing pushes 3σg above 1πᵤ, so the last two of N₂'s 10 valence electrons go into 3σg.

B₂ is paramagnetic. Why is this evidence for s–p mixing?
Elements: B
* With mixing, 3σg rises above 1πᵤ, so B₂'s last two electrons go singly into the two 1πᵤ orbitals
- Without mixing, B₂ would have no bonding electrons
- s–p mixing adds an extra electron to B₂
- Mixing turns 1πᵤ into an antibonding orbital
# Explanation: Without mixing, 3σg would be the HOMO, doubly occupied, and B₂ would have no unpaired electrons.

How does valence bond theory deal with s orbitals interacting with p orbitals?
Elements: C
- It ignores them completely
* It assumes the s and p orbitals of an atom hybridise (e.g. sp³) before bonding
- It treats every bond as ionic
- It adds extra electrons to the s orbitals
# Explanation: You'll meet sp, sp² and sp³ hybrid orbitals in organic chemistry. MO theory reaches a similar result by a different route.

In a heteronuclear diatomic, a molecular orbital is much closer in energy to one atom's orbital than the other's. What follows?
* It has a larger contribution from that orbital, and its electrons are mostly on that atom
- It has a larger contribution from the other atom's orbital
- Its electrons are shared exactly equally
- It must be a non-bonding orbital
# Explanation: That is how MO theory explains bond polarity.

In LiH, both electrons occupy a σ bonding orbital that is very similar to hydrogen's 1s orbital. What does this tell you?
Elements: Li
- The bond is perfectly covalent
* The bond has high ionic character: it is almost Li⁺H⁻
- It is almost Li⁻H⁺
- LiH has no bond
# Explanation: It is almost as if lithium simply gave its electron to hydrogen.

In LiH, which atomic orbital contributes most to the σ antibonding orbital?
Elements: Li
* Li 2s
- H 1s
- Li 1s
- Both equally
# Explanation: The antibonding orbital is close in energy to Li 2s, which is higher than H 1s, so it looks mostly like the Li 2s orbital.

In HF, why does fluorine's 2s orbital stay non-bonding?
Elements: F
* It is too far below the H 1s orbital in energy to interact
- It has the wrong symmetry
- It is empty
- It is too far away in space
# Explanation: Similar energy is needed for a strong interaction. F 2s is much lower than H 1s.

In HF, why do fluorine's 2pₓ and 2py orbitals stay non-bonding?
Elements: F
* They have the wrong symmetry to interact with hydrogen's 1s orbital
- They are too far from H 1s in energy
- They are empty
- They bond with each other instead
# Explanation: Their in-phase and out-of-phase overlaps with H 1s cancel. Only F 2pz, pointing at the hydrogen, can interact.

In a diatomic, can an s orbital on one atom interact with the pz orbital on the other?
- No: s and p orbitals can never interact
- Only to form π orbitals
* Yes: it forms σ bonding and σ antibonding orbitals
- Only if both atoms are the same element
# Explanation: pz points along the axis towards the s orbital, so they can overlap end-on. s can't interact with pₓ or py.

How are electrons in non-bonding orbitals counted when working out bond order?
- As bonding electrons
- As antibonding electrons
* They are ignored
- As half a bonding electron each
# Explanation: HF: 2 bonding, 0 antibonding (the six non-bonding electrons don't count), so the bond order is 1.

In molecular orbital terms, why is the H–F bond polar?
Elements: H
- The σ bonding orbital has a bigger contribution from H 1s
- Hydrogen's electron goes into a non-bonding orbital
- The bonding orbital is empty
* The filled σ bonding orbital has a bigger contribution from the lower-energy F 2pz, so its electrons are mostly on F
# Explanation: Fluorine is more electronegative, so its 2p orbitals are lower in energy than H 1s: F is δ−.

What bond order does molecular orbital theory give carbon monoxide?
Elements: C
- 2
- 2½
* 3
- 1
# Explanation: 10 valence electrons fill four bonding orbitals (8 electrons) and one antibonding orbital (2): (8 − 2) ÷ 2 = 3, matching the Lewis triple bond.

In the molecular orbital diagram of CO, which atom's valence atomic orbitals are lower in energy?
Elements: O
- Carbon
* Oxygen
- Neither: they are at the same energy
# Explanation: Oxygen is more electronegative. C and O orbitals are fairly close in energy, so they still interact strongly, with s–p mixing as in N₂.
