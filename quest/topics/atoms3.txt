Which observation shows that a beam of electrons behaves as a wave?
* Electron diffraction: the beam gives an interference pattern
- Electrons are deflected by a magnet
- Electrons carry a negative charge
- Electrons are ejected from metals by UV light
# Explanation: Diffraction and interference (constructive and destructive) are wave behaviours. A stream of electrons shows them, just as light does.

The wavelengths of electron beams are typically similar to…
- the wavelength of visible light
* the lengths of bonds between atoms
- the width of a human hair
- the size of an atomic nucleus
# Explanation: Because the wavelengths are comparable to bond lengths, electron diffraction can be used to measure bond lengths in molecules.

C₆₀ "buckyball" molecules have been shown to diffract. What does this demonstrate?
Elements: C
- Carbon atoms have no mass
* Even large molecules show wave-like behaviour
- Only charged particles behave as waves
- C₆₀ molecules are smaller than electrons
# Explanation: In theory any moving object has a wavelength. C₆₀ is among the largest objects in which wave behaviour has been observed experimentally.

An electron held in an atom behaves as a standing wave. Why does this lead to quantised energy levels?
- The electron slows down as it orbits
- The nucleus absorbs some wavelengths
* Only certain wavelengths fit around the atom
- Standing waves have no energy
# Explanation: The wave has to join up with itself all the way round. Only some wavelengths do this, and each allowed pattern corresponds to a value of n and an energy.

Which statement best describes an electron in an atom?
- It follows a circular path around the nucleus
- It sits still at a fixed distance from the nucleus
- It jumps between fixed points on a circle
* It exists as the whole wave around the atom at once, more in some places than others
# Explanation: Diagrams of standing waves are not pictures of the electron's path. The wave is the electron.

What is an electron's wavefunction, ψ?
- The path the electron follows around the nucleus
- The energy needed to remove the electron
- The speed of the electron at each moment
* A mathematical function that gives a value at every point in space around the nucleus
# Explanation: Feed in a position (x, y, z) and the wavefunction gives out a number. In the lecture, ψ(0.1, 0.2, 0.1) = 0.44 for hydrogen's 1s electron.

A wavefunction can be positive in one region and negative in another. What does the sign tell you?
- Whether the electron is there or not
* The phase of the wave in that region
- The charge of the electron there
- Whether that region is inside or outside the atom
# Explanation: Positive and negative regions are out of phase with each other. The sign matters when orbitals combine to form bonds.

The probability of finding an electron in a small region is proportional to…
- ψ
* ψ² (the wavefunction squared)
- √ψ
- 1/ψ
# Explanation: Squaring removes the sign: |ψ|² is always positive or zero, as a probability must be.

At one point ψ = +0.4 and at another ψ = −0.4. How do the chances of finding the electron at the two points compare?
- It is more likely at the +0.4 point
* They are equal
- It is more likely at the −0.4 point
- The electron can't be at the −0.4 point
# Explanation: Probability depends on ψ², and (+0.4)² = (−0.4)² = 0.16. The sign is phase, not likelihood.

Can we work out exactly where an electron is at a particular moment?
* No: we can only find the probability of it being in a region
- Yes, from its wavefunction
- Yes, if we know its energy
- Only for the 1s orbital
# Explanation: It is fundamentally impossible to pin down an electron's position. The best we can do is a probability, for example 12% that it is inside a particular cube.

Can electron density ever be negative?
- Yes, wherever ψ is negative
* No: it is always positive or zero
- Yes, at a node
- Only in p orbitals
# Explanation: Electron density follows ψ², which is never negative, even where ψ itself is negative. Unlike ψ, density has no phase.

An orbital is drawn as a "balloon" (boundary surface). Is the electron contained inside that surface?
- Yes: the electron cannot leave the surface
* No: the surface marks an arbitrary value of ψ, and the wavefunction extends beyond it
- Yes, but only in the ground state
- No: the electron is only ever on the surface itself
# Explanation: The lecture's example used a threshold of ±0.5. Choosing ±0.25 would give bigger balloons for the same orbital. Wavefunctions approach zero far from the nucleus but never reach a hard edge.

What are the two main things you can get from an atom's wavefunction?
- Its charge and its mass
* Where the electron is likely to be, and its energy
- Its speed and its direction of travel
- Its age and its spin
# Explanation: The wavefunction gives the probability distribution (so electron density) and the electron's energy.

Why is there no 1p orbital?
- 1p orbitals exist but are too high in energy
* For n = 1, l can only be 0, which is an s orbital
- p orbitals can only hold one electron
- The 1p orbital is the same as 2s
# Explanation: l can be any whole number from 0 to n − 1. For n = 1 that is just l = 0 (s).

Which letter is used for an orbital with l = 2?
- p
* d
- f
- s
# Explanation: s, p, d and f stand for l = 0, 1, 2 and 3.

What does the principal quantum number n mostly tell you about an orbital?
* Its energy level and how far it extends from the nucleus
- Its shape
- Its orientation in space
- The direction of the electron's spin
# Explanation: Higher n means a higher energy level and a more spread-out wavefunction. Shape is set by l and orientation by mₗ.

Which quantum number sets the shape of an orbital (s, p, d, f)?
- n, the principal quantum number
* l, the secondary quantum number
- mₗ, the magnetic quantum number
- mₛ, the spin quantum number
# Explanation: l gives the subshell letter and so the shape. mₗ picks out the individual orbitals within it, such as the three 2p orbitals.

What is an angular node (nodal plane)?
* A plane through the nucleus where ψ = 0, so the electron is never found there
- The point where the electron is most likely to be
- The outer edge of the orbital
- A plane where ψ is largest
# Explanation: On one side of the plane ψ is positive and on the other negative. Because ψ varies smoothly, it must pass through zero at the plane.

In a 2pz orbital, how do the two lobes above and below the xy plane differ?
- One contains the electron and the other doesn't
- One is much larger than the other
* They have opposite signs of ψ (opposite phases)
- They are identical in every way
# Explanation: ψ is positive above the xy plane and negative below it, with a nodal plane between. ψ² is the same shape in both lobes.

What does a radial distribution function (RDF) show?
* How likely the electron is to be found at a given distance from the nucleus, in any direction
- The shape of the orbital
- How the electron's energy changes with time
- The sign of the wavefunction at each point
# Explanation: The peak of the RDF is the most likely distance. Larger n puts the peak further out.

At about what distance from the nucleus is the peak of hydrogen's 1s radial distribution function?
Elements: H
- 0 pm
* 53 pm
- 5.3 pm
- 530 pm
# Explanation: The peak, about 53 pm, is the most likely distance. The RDF is zero at the nucleus itself.

Which has the greater chance of being found very close to the nucleus?
- A 2p electron
* A 2s electron
- They are the same
- Neither: both have zero probability close to the nucleus
# Explanation: The 2s RDF has a small inner hump close to the nucleus; 2p has none. Next lecture uses this to explain why 2s is lower in energy than 2p.

In a one-electron atom such as hydrogen, how do the energies of the 2s and 2p orbitals compare?
Elements: H
- 2s is lower
- 2p is lower
- 2p doesn't exist in hydrogen
* They are the same (degenerate)
# Explanation: With only one electron, the energy depends only on n, proportional to −1/n².

In atoms with more than one electron, how do the 2s and 2p subshells compare in energy?
- They are the same
* 2s is lower than 2p
- 2p is lower than 2s
- 2s is higher than 3s
# Explanation: With more than one electron, the subshells within a shell split apart in energy. Next lecture explains why.

What does the Pauli exclusion principle say?
* No two electrons in an atom may have exactly the same set of quantum numbers
- Electrons always fill the highest orbital first
- Each orbital can hold any number of electrons
- Electrons in the same atom must have the same spin
# Explanation: This is why electrons can't all crowd into 1s: each orbital takes at most two, with opposite spins.

Helium has two electrons in its 1s orbital. How can that satisfy the Pauli exclusion principle?
Elements: He
- They have different values of n
* They have different spin quantum numbers, +½ and −½
- One is in a 1p orbital
- The principle doesn't apply to helium
# Explanation: Both have n = 1, l = 0, mₗ = 0. The fourth quantum number, mₛ, differs: one ↑ (+½), one ↓ (−½).

What values can the spin quantum number mₛ take?
- 0 or 1
* +½ or −½
- −1, 0 or +1
- Any value from −l to +l
# Explanation: Spin has only two directions, drawn as ↑ (+½) and ↓ (−½).

Why does lithium's third electron go into the 2s orbital?
Elements: Li
- 2s is lower in energy than 1s
- Lithium has no 1s orbital
* The 1s orbital is full (one ↑ and one ↓), and 2s is the next lowest in energy
- Electrons always go into the largest orbital
# Explanation: Pauli allows only two electrons in 1s. The next one goes into the lowest-energy orbital with a vacancy, which is 2s.

Which set of quantum numbers could describe lithium's outermost (2s) electron?
Elements: Li
- n = 1, l = 0, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½
* n = 2, l = 0, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½
- n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½
- n = 2, l = 0, mₗ = 1, mₛ = +½
# Explanation: 2s means n = 2 and l = 0, so mₗ can only be 0. The first option is 1s, the third is 2p, and the fourth isn't allowed because mₗ can't exceed l.

Why is helium's 1s orbital lower in energy than hydrogen's?
Elements: He
- Helium has more neutrons
- Helium's 1s orbital holds more electrons
* Helium's nucleus has a greater positive charge
- Helium is a noble gas
# Explanation: As the nuclear charge increases, the "same" orbitals become smaller and lower in energy.

In the configuration 1s² 2s¹, what does the superscript 2 mean?
- The 1s orbital is squared
* Two electrons are in the 1s subshell
- It is the second shell
- There are two 1s orbitals
# Explanation: 1s² 2s¹ (lithium) means two electrons in 1s and one in 2s.
